# Wine Faults: A Comprehensive Guide to Diagnosis and Service

Wine faults are flaws that emerge during production, storage, or transport—defects that genuinely diminish the wine's quality. Understanding them helps you distinguish between a genuinely faulty bottle and a wine that's simply not your style. This guide walks you through the major faults, how to recognize them, and what to do when you encounter them.

## Cork Taint (TCA)

### What It Is and What It Smells Like

Cork taint is caused by 2,4,6-trichloroanisole, commonly known as TCA, a chemical compound that develops when chlorine comes into contact with naturally occurring molds in cork bark. The result is unmistakable: a distinctly musty, moldy aroma reminiscent of wet cardboard, a damp basement, or a mildewy dishcloth. Some people describe it as a moldy newspaper smell. It's not a subtle fault—if cork taint is present, it's usually fairly obvious.

### How Common Is It?

For many years, the industry cited a 3–5% incidence rate, but more recent data suggests the problem is decreasing. A 2016 review found taint in about 6.2% of tasted cork-sealed wines, but by 2020 that number had dropped to 2.78%, settling around 3% by 2021. Modern cork processing has improved significantly, though cork taint remains the most common single wine fault.

### The Chemistry Behind It

TCA forms through a complex interaction: chlorine (used to disinfect cork during processing) reacts with molds naturally present in cork bark. Bacteria then create the TCA compound through a process called microbial O-methylation. The result is so potent that trained wine professionals can detect it at concentrations as low as 1–2 nanograms per liter—extraordinarily sensitive to the human nose.

### How It's Not About Cork Bits

A common source of confusion: cork taint has nothing to do with bits of cork floating in your wine. If you're seeing cork particles, that's a minor cosmetic issue caused by a damaged cork during opening. That wine is still fine to drink (you can decant it to remove the particles). Cork taint is purely about chemical contamination of the entire bottle, making the wine undrinkable at any concentration.

### How to Confirm It

The key diagnostic test: **the wine gets worse with air exposure, not better.** If you've decanted a wine or opened it and left it for a few hours, watch for the musty notes. Real cork taint intensifies or remains constant; it never "blows off." In a restaurant, smell the cork itself—it may have a musty odor. But the final confirmation comes from tasting: that wet cardboard character dominates the palate and doesn't improve.

## Oxidation

### The Fault

Oxidation occurs when wine has been exposed to excess oxygen—typically through a defective cork, faulty closure, or improper storage. Unlike the deliberate, controlled oxidation used in producing certain wines (Sherry, Madeira, some Jura wines), unwanted oxidation is a serious flaw that strips a wine of its freshness and vibrance.

### How to Recognize It Visually and Aromatically

**Color**: A white wine that shows excessive amber, gold, or even brown discoloration. Red wines turn orange, rust, or brown rather than deep purple. **Aroma**: Loss of the wine's fresh fruit character. Instead, you'll notice: flat or stewed apple, apple cider, caramel, or nut-like notes (reminiscent of windfall fruit). The wine smells tired and oxidized, lacking the crispness it should have. **Taste**: The wine tastes flat, lacking acidity and freshness. The fruit flavors seem muted or cooked out entirely.

### The Deliberately Oxidative Exception

A few wine styles are meant to be oxidized: Fino and Amontillado Sherry, Madeira, Vin Jaune from Jura, and some traditional Austrian whites develop their character through deliberate oxidative aging. These are intentionally made this way and are delicious. The key difference is that in a fault, oxidation steals complexity; in a deliberate style, it creates it.

### Why It Happens

Oxidation usually points to a closure failure. A cork that's dried out, cracked, or porous will allow oxygen to seep in over time. Poor storage conditions—exposure to heat or sunlight—accelerate the process. Some wines oxidize faster than others; white wines and light reds lack the tannins and phenolic compounds that protect red wines from oxidation.

## Reduction and Reduced Aromas

### What It Smells Like

A "reduced" wine is one made with minimal oxygen exposure during winemaking. While some reduction can add complexity and freshness (especially in cool-climate whites and some reds), excessive reduction creates decidedly unpleasant aromas: struck match, rotten eggs, rubber, fresh tire smell, rotten cabbage, or sewage-like notes. These come from volatile sulfur compounds called mercaptans.

### The Good News: It's Often Fixable

Here's what makes reduction unique among wine faults: **it's frequently reversible.** Mercaptan compounds respond beautifully to aeration. Simply swirling the wine vigorously or decanting it can allow those stinky compounds to "blow off" within 5–30 minutes.

### How to Test and Recover

1. **Decant aggressively** into a wide-bottomed decanter and let it sit for 20–30 minutes, or longer if the smell is severe.
2. **Swirl vigorously** in your glass for 2–3 minutes.
3. **Smell again** after a few minutes of air contact. The struck match or rotten egg notes should diminish or disappear.
4. **If it persists past 5–10 minutes**, the reduction is likely permanent; the wine is irreversibly flawed.

### A Winemaker's Trick

If you have a penny minted before 1982 (it contains copper), dropping it into your glass or decanter can help mercaptan compounds dissipate. The copper chemically binds with the sulfur, eliminating the smell—a neat, if unconventional, solution.

### When Reduction Adds Complexity

A touch of reduction—just a whisper of struck match or fresh rubber in a young white Burgundy or Alsatian Riesling—isn't a fault; it's a sign of careful, minimalist winemaking. The key word is "touch." If the aromas are dominant and unpleasant rather than subtle and interesting, you're looking at a fault.

## Volatile Acidity (VA)

### What It Smells and Tastes Like

Volatile acidity is the wine's measure of volatile (gaseous) acids, primarily acetic acid and its ester, ethyl acetate. At low levels, VA adds lift and complexity to a wine. But at high levels, it's unmistakable: nail polish remover, vinegar, or sharp solvent-like aromas that dominate the palate and overwhelm the fruit. The taste is sharp, sour, and unpleasant—a harsh vinegary burn on the finish.

### The Detection Threshold

The sensory threshold where most people begin to detect VA as a fault is around 0.7 grams per liter. At 1.4 g/L, the wine smells and tastes unmistakably like vinegar. However, this is highly variable—some wines show VA at lower concentrations, others can hide it even at higher levels, depending on the wine's other characteristics and your own sensitivity.

### The Complexity of Perception

Here's where it gets interesting: not everyone tastes VA the same way. Some people detect sharp, vinegary notes in wines where others simply experience heightened acidity and freshness. A little VA can be perceived as elegance and lift; too much is unquestionably a fault. The legal maximum is 0.14 g/100 mL for red wines and 0.12 g/100 mL for white wines in most regions.

### What Causes It

VA typically develops during fermentation or storage, usually indicating bacterial spoilage (acetic acid bacteria) or exposure to oxygen. It's most common in wines that have been exposed to heat, poor sanitation, or in open containers. Winemakers monitor free sulfite levels to help prevent VA, as sulfur dioxide inhibits the bacteria that create it.

## Brettanomyces (Brett)

### The Aromas: Fascinating or Faulty?

Brettanomyces bruxellensis is a yeast that produces volatile phenols—compounds that create unmistakable aromas: barnyard, horse stable, sweaty saddle, band-aid (iodoform), leather, or smoky tobacco notes. In small amounts, these can add rustic, earthy complexity. In larger amounts, they're simply musty and unpleasant.

### The Great Debate: When Is Brett a Fault?

This is the most contentious fault in wine. Unlike cork taint, which is universally recognized as a defect, Brett sits in a gray zone. Many old-world winemakers—particularly in Bordeaux and the Rhône Valley—view a little Brett as a feature, not a bug. It's part of the wine's rustic character.

**The general rule**: If you enjoy the barnyard/leather notes and they add to the wine's complexity without overwhelming it, it's character. If the sweaty saddle smell is dominant and off-putting, it's a fault. Regional and personal tolerance for Brett varies enormously.

### How to Detect It

The volatile phenols that characterize Brett are most noticeable on the first sniff, especially in warm glasses. Swirl and sniff carefully. The aromas should be identifiable: wet horse, leather, or band-aid-like notes. It's rarely subtle.

### Controlling Brett in Production

Winemakers prevent Brett through strict sanitation, SO₂ additions, and careful barrel management. One effective monitoring technique is measuring the ratio of free to total sulfite: if Brett or other microorganisms are active in a barrel, they'll consume the free sulfite, lowering the ratio and signaling that treatment is needed.

## Heat Damage (Cooked Wine)

### How to Spot It Before Opening

**Visual signs** are often your first warning:
- A cork that protrudes slightly or sticks out from the bottle (heat expansion)
- A sticky residue or weeping wine around the cork or capsule
- A capsule that looks swollen or damaged
- For white wines, a color that's unusually golden, amber, or even brown

These are red flags that the wine has been exposed to sustained heat.

### What Cooked Wine Tastes Like

Heat damage creates immediately recognizable aromas and flavors: stewed or baked fruit without freshness, sometimes burnt notes, caramel, or jammy, overcooked character. The wine's bright fruit flavors collapse into dull, flat, overripe notes. It tastes like the fruit has been cooked in a slow oven—which, in a sense, it has.

### Temperature and Timeline

The critical thresholds: sustained temperatures above 70°F (21°C) will gradually damage wine. At 80°F (27°C), wine begins to "cook" noticeably. Above 86°F (30°C), serious damage occurs within 24 hours. At 100°F (38°C), a wine can be ruined in just 6 hours.

### Why It Matters

Heat damage is largely irreversible. Unlike reduction, which responds to aeration, cooked wines cannot be fixed by opening them. The chemical structure of the wine has been altered. This is why proper transportation and storage—especially in warm climates—is so critical for wine quality.

## Light Strike

### The Problem with UV Light

Light strike occurs when UV light damages the wine's compounds, creating unpleasant, off aromas and flavors. It can happen surprisingly quickly: as little as 60 minutes of direct sunlight on a clear glass bottle in bright light can cause light strike in delicate wines. Sparkling wines are especially vulnerable because the bubbles seem to exaggerate and carry the fault more prominently.

### Bottle Color Protection

**Clear glass**: Offers minimal protection, blocking only 10% of damaging UV wavelengths. Clear glass bottles are risky unless wrapped (as with Cristal Champagne, which comes in protective cellophane).

**Green glass**: Varies significantly, but typically blocks 50–90% of harmful rays depending on hue, darkness, and thickness. Most standard wine bottles use green glass for a reason.

**Brown or amber glass**: Nearly opaque, providing the best protection—blocking 90% of harmful light and 99% of UV radiation specifically. Most beer bottles are brown for this reason.

### Why Champagne Often Comes in Clear Glass

Some prestige Champagne houses deliberately use clear glass as a marketing statement (Louis Roederer's Cristal is the famous example). The trade-off: they must protect bottles with cellophane wrapping or ensure rapid turnover. More producers are switching to brown glass bottles for genuine protection.

## Volatile Sulfur and Over-Sulfiting

### Too Little Sulfur: Premature Oxidation

Sulfur dioxide (SO₂) is essential in winemaking—it prevents oxidation and microbes. Wines with insufficient SO₂ can oxidize prematurely, especially if stored improperly. You'll notice accelerated browning, flat fruit, and the tired flavors of oxidation appearing far too early.

### Too Much Sulfur: Burned Match or Numbing Aromas

Excessive SO₂ creates a burnt match or striking match aroma. At very high levels, it can numb your palate and suppress the wine's fruit character. This is rare in professional winemaking but can occur if a winemaker is overly cautious with SO₂ additions.

### The Balance

Winemakers walk a tightrope: enough SO₂ to protect the wine, not so much that it becomes a sensory fault. Well-made wines shouldn't have any obvious SO₂ character. If you smell it prominently, there's a balance issue.

## Refermentation in Bottle

### What It Looks and Tastes Like

A previously still wine that becomes unexpectedly fizzy or lightly carbonated is experiencing refermentation in the bottle. You might notice: small fizz on the palate, visible bubbles or sediment, or in extreme cases, a cork that's partially pushed out due to pressure buildup.

### What Causes It

Refermentation happens when the wine wasn't fully dry when bottled (still containing fermentable sugars) and wasn't properly stabilized. Later, either:
- **Residual yeast** reawakens and continues fermenting those sugars, or
- **Malolactic bacteria** converts malic acid into lactic acid and CO₂, creating unwanted carbonation

### Why It's a Problem

The wine tastes wrong for its style. It's supposed to be still; instead, it's fizzy. This can also create pressure inside the bottle, risking cork pop or, in extreme cases, bottle breakage.

### Prevention

Winemakers prevent this by fermenting wines to complete dryness (specific gravity below 0.995) and stabilizing with sulfur dioxide or other methods before bottling. Home winemakers sometimes struggle with this, which is why freshly made still wines occasionally carbonate in bottle.

## What's NOT a Fault: Tartrate Crystals and Sediment

### Wine Diamonds: Harmless and Actually a Good Sign

Those small, crystal-like deposits you find in a bottle of white wine or stuck to the inside of a cork are tartrates—potassium bitartrate crystals formed when tartaric acid (naturally present in all wine) reacts with potassium. They're affectionately called "wine diamonds," and they're completely harmless.

In fact, **their presence is often a sign of quality.** Winemakers who skip cold stabilization (a process where wine is chilled to force these crystals out before bottling) retain more natural acidity, freshness, and age-worthiness. Modern sommeliers and quality-focused winemakers view tartrate crystals as an indicator of careful, minimally processed winemaking.

You can brush them off, let them dissolve on your tongue—they taste like nothing—or simply leave them be. They're never a fault, and they never affect the wine's quality.

### Sediment in Aged Wines

Red wines, especially those aged 8+ years, often develop dark sediment—tannins, color compounds, and other solids that naturally precipitate over time. This is not a fault; it's a hallmark of properly aged wine. It's actually reassuring evidence that the wine wasn't over-filtered or over-processed.

When serving an older wine, decant it slowly and carefully to separate the clear wine from the sediment at the bottom. The sediment itself is harmless but gritty—no one wants to bite down on it. Pouring carefully avoids that issue entirely.

## Screw Caps vs. Cork: A Fault Perspective

### Cork's Liability

Natural cork is an organic material, which means it varies slightly from cork to cork. It's also vulnerable to TCA contamination and can dry out, losing its seal. The industry estimates that cork-sealed wines suffer some form of taint in about 3–5% of cases, with TCA being the most common culprit.

### Screw Cap's Advantages (and Minor Issues)

Screw caps provide a reliable, consistent seal that prevents cork taint entirely. They're increasingly favored by quality producers, especially of white wines and aromatics where freshness is paramount.

The downsides are minimal: screw-capped wines occasionally develop reduction (that struck-match aroma) because the seal is too airtight, allowing no oxygen ingress. This is rare and usually resolves with decanting. "Flint strike" (a metallic taste) can occasionally occur, but it's uncommon and not nearly as prevalent as cork taint.

Mechanically, screw caps are more durable. A dropped case won't compromise the seal the way cork can.

### The Modern Consensus

Most quality-conscious winemakers now favor screw caps for their reliability, especially for wines meant to be drunk young. The "screw cap = cheap wine" prejudice has entirely disappeared in professional circles. Some producers use hybrid closures combining cork and synthetics, but the trend is unmistakably toward screw caps for consistent quality.

## The Restaurant Wine Service Ritual: How to Evaluate a Wine Properly

### Why You Taste First

When a server pours you a small taste of wine before serving the entire table, they're not asking for approval of your palate—they're asking you to confirm the wine is not faulty. This protects everyone: you get to check the bottle before committing to it, and the restaurant knows the wine is good before it's poured for others.

### The Three-Step Evaluation

**1. Look**: Hold the wine up to light and examine the color. Look for clarity (cloudiness could signal refermentation or other issues). Check for any sediment. In general, the color should be what you expect for the wine's style and age.

**2. Smell**: Swirl gently to aerate, then bring the glass to your nose. Concentrate on what you detect. Are there any musty, cardboard-like notes (cork taint)? Any obviously off or unpleasant aromas? The first smell is often the most telling; let yourself react honestly.

**3. Taste**: Take a small sip, let it coat your mouth, and focus on whether anything seems obviously wrong. A tiny sip is enough—you're checking for faults, not tasting for flavor profile.

### Red Flags That Warrant Sending the Wine Back

- **Wet cardboard, musty basement smell**: Cork taint
- **Obvious nail polish remover or vinegar**: High volatile acidity
- **Rotten egg or rubber smell that doesn't blow off quickly**: Irreversible reduction
- **Obvious brown color with oxidized apple/cider aromas**: Oxidation
- **Cooked, stewed fruit character**: Heat damage
- **Any obvious unpleasant aroma that dominates the wine**: Send it back

### What NOT to Send Back

You cannot send back a wine because you don't like it or because it's not what you expected. Wine service isn't a judgment on your taste—it's about quality assurance. A wine can be perfectly healthy and delicious even if it's a style you dislike. If the wine is technically sound (no obvious faults), you've committed to the bottle.

### Timing Reality

Ideally, you identify a faulty bottle immediately during your tasting. In practice, you might not detect cork taint until 10–20 minutes into drinking, after the wine has warmed and aerated. Don't feel self-conscious about flagging it then. A good server and restaurant will still replace it—the fault is the wine's problem, not yours.

## When to Return a Bottle: Retail vs. Restaurant

### At a Restaurant

Once you've ordered and the wine has been opened at the table, most restaurants will replace a genuinely faulty bottle without argument. Recognize that this policy protects you: they want you to enjoy your meal. Be honest and straightforward: "This tastes corked" or "I'm detecting significant oxidation." Good restaurants appreciate the heads-up.

If you've downed most of the bottle and then complain, expect pushback—the restaurant can reasonably argue they can't prove the fault existed in their sample. But if you flag it early, you're in the right.

### At a Retail Shop

If you purchase a bottle at a wine shop and discover it's faulty when you get home, bring it back. Most quality wine shops will replace it immediately—they have reputation and professional standing to maintain. Bring the bottle (even partially empty is fine) and explain the issue clearly.

Don't expect a refund for a wine you simply didn't like or that turned out to be from a vintage you dislike. Retail returns are for genuine defects: cork taint, oxidation, or other faults beyond the consumer's control.

## Distinguishing Style from Fault

The hardest question for newer wine drinkers: Is this wine faulty, or is it just not my style?

**A style question**: The wine is clean and sound, but you find it too dry, too acidic, too tannic, too full-bodied, or simply not your preference. Many wonderful wines aren't enjoyable to everyone. This isn't a fault—it's a mismatch.

**A fault**: The wine has an obvious flaw—musty, vinegary, cooked, or otherwise broken. It tastes damaged or spoiled, not just different from your preferences.

Here's the practical rule: **If the first thing you notice is an off, unpleasant, or strange aroma or flavor that dominates the wine, it's probably a fault.** If the wine tastes clean and complete but just isn't to your liking, it's a style question. Trust your initial instinct. Your nose is usually right.

---

## Final Thoughts

Wine faults are real and worth understanding, but they're also relatively rare in quality bottles from reputable sources. The vast majority of wines you drink will be sound. Knowing what to look—and smell—for simply gives you confidence and clarity when you encounter a genuinely flawed bottle.

The good news: most faults are obvious. A freshly corked wine, an oxidized white, an aggressively reduced red—these announce themselves. Your job is simply to notice, evaluate honestly, and act accordingly. In a restaurant, communicate clearly. At home, learn what you can from the experience.

And remember: tartrate crystals, sediment, and a little reduction are not the enemy. They're often signs of honest, carefully made wine. The real faults—the ones that genuinely diminish quality—are the ones that taste broken, not the ones that taste different.
