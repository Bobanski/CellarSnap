# Italian Wine: A Sommelier's Guide

Italy is the world's most diverse wine-producing nation—not because of size, but because of philosophy. While France built its reputation on strict, narrow definitions of excellence, Italy embraced complexity, regional identity, and the courage to break its own rules. This flexibility has created everything from Europe's most elegant age-worthy reds to some of the most exciting natural wines being made today.

Understanding Italian wine means understanding how to navigate a system that rewards both tradition and innovation, where the smallest villages produce wines that rival the world's most prestigious bottles, and where a wine that breaks the rules can become more famous than wines that follow them perfectly.

## Italian Wine Law: DOCG, DOC, IGT, and Why Super Tuscans Changed Everything

Italy's wine classification system, established in 1963 and modeled after France's AOC system, creates a four-tier hierarchy that tells you something crucial about what's in the bottle—but not always what you'd expect.

**DOCG** (Denominazione di Origine Controllata e Garantita) is the highest tier. These wines must satisfy stringent requirements covering grape varieties, minimum aging, alcohol content, and harvest yields. Before sale, every DOCG wine is tasted and approved by a government panel and sealed with a numbered governmental strip to prevent tampering. Italy now has 74 DOCG designations, most concentrated in Piedmont, Tuscany, and the Veneto. When you see DOCG on a label, you're looking at a wine made within strict, legally defined parameters by producers who've chosen to work within the system.

**DOC** (Denominazione di Origine Controllata) sits below DOCG and requires compliance with regional rules about grape varieties and production methods, but with slightly less stringent oversight than DOCG. A wine like Barolo is DOCG; a smaller Piedmont appellation might be DOC. Both are serious, traditional wines—the difference is prestige and regulation intensity.

**IGT** (Indicazione Geografica Tipica) was created in 1992 specifically to legitimize what had become Italy's most rebellious wines. These are high-quality wines that refuse to follow traditional rules. They might use non-traditional grapes, break the blending requirements, or be aged in ways that violate DOCG specifications. IGT simply says "this wine is from this region, and it's good, but the producer doesn't want to follow our traditional rules."

**The Super Tuscan Revolution** happened because Tuscan producers grew frustrated with Chianti's rigid requirements. In the 1960s and 70s, visionary winemakers like Marchesi Antinori began planting Cabernet Sauvignon, Merlot, and Cabernet Franc alongside traditional Sangiovese—or replacing it entirely. Their wines, like Antinori's Tignanello (a Sangiovese-Cabernet blend from 1971), became world-famous and commanded prices equal to or higher than top Chianti. But these wines couldn't legally be called Chianti because Chianti rules required minimum Sangiovese percentages. So they were released as IGT—technically "lower" classification, but practically revolutionary. This forced Italian wine law to evolve and acknowledge that innovation could coexist with tradition.

Today, IGT is no longer seen as lesser; it's seen as a choice. Some producers use it proudly to signal that they're making their vision, not following rules. Others use it because their vineyard sits outside traditional zones. The classification tells you the producer's philosophy as much as the wine's quality.

## Piedmont: Where Tradition and Modernism Still Debate

Piedmont (Piemonte) sits in the misty hills of northwest Italy, bordering France and Switzerland. It's the kingdom of Nebbiolo, a temperamental, tannic red grape that produces Italy's most age-worthy and most debated wines. The regional wine culture is deeply traditional—old families, old cellars, old certainties—but Piedmont's modern revolution was fierce and left visible scars still being reconciled today.

### Barolo: The "King of Wines"

Barolo holds DOCG status and must be made from 100% Nebbiolo grapes grown in the Barolo zone, a small collection of communes south of Alba. The wine requires minimum 38 months of aging with at least 18 months in oak. Barolo is structured, powerful, and often austere when young. Tannins dominate the first decade—think leather, tar, dried plum, licorice, and rose. These wines commonly need 15-20 years to achieve harmony, though modern styles can be more approachable at 5-8 years.

The soil matters enormously. The Barolo DOCG zone sits on three distinct geological areas. The sandy, calcareous soils of Serralunga produce the most tannic, structured wines with the longest aging potential. The clay-rich soils around Barolo town produce softer, more aromatic wines. The limestone-heavy Cannubi zone produces wines of incredible elegance. A knowledgeable consumer will notice these differences long before understanding production philosophies.

Barolo's winemaking split into two camps in the 1980s and 90s. The traditionalists (like Bartolo Mascarello and Luciano Sandrone) favored long maceration periods (20-30 days), large neutral old oak (botti), and extended bottle age before release. The modernists—called the "Barolo Boys" (Elio Altare, Gianfranco Bovio, and others)—used shorter maceration (7-10 days), smaller French barriques, and lower aging temperatures to preserve bright fruit flavors. The traditional style yielded wines that were almost black in youth and seemed austere. The modern style was immediately voluptuous.

By the early 2020s, the pendulum has swung toward synthesis. Contemporary winemakers are rediscovering traditional long maceration techniques but adapting them with modern temperature control. They use a mix of large and small oak. They preserve freshness without sacrificing structure. The best modern Barolos taste like a conversation between the old guard and the new wave—they have the tannins and aging potential of tradition with the fruit clarity and approachability of modernity. Barolo's current regional style is arguably stronger than either extreme.

Seek out producers like Mascarello, Sandrone, Bruno Giacosa, Paolo Scavino, and Falletto. Expect to pay $40-80 for solid examples, $100+ for prestigious names and older vintages.

### Barbaresco: Barolo's More Approachable Sibling

Barbaresco is made from the same Nebbiolo grape in a neighboring zone and holds equal DOCG status. The key difference is terroir and timing. Barbaresco's slightly warmer microclimate ripens grapes faster, producing wines with slightly lower acidity and slightly higher alcohol. The mandatory aging is shorter (26 months instead of 38, with 9 months minimum in oak instead of 18).

The practical result: Barbaresco is more approachable younger than Barolo, though equally complex with age. A 7-year-old Barbaresco is often more enjoyable than a 7-year-old Barolo. By 15-20 years, the best examples achieve similar complexity. Serve at 55°F, preferably after decanting.

Classic Barbaresco producers include Gaja (which redefined the category in the 1980s with a more modern, fruit-forward style), Produttori del Barbaresco (a cooperative making excellent traditional wine), Bruno Rocca, and Pio Cesare. Barbaresco is often 15-30% cheaper than comparable Barolo from the same vintage, making it an excellent value play for elegant Nebbiolo.

### Barbera: The Grape Piedmont Drinks at Lunch

Nebbiolo demands reverence. Barbera demands affection. This is the workhorse red of Piedmont—the wine locals drink, the wine that's never pretentious, and increasingly, the wine that produces some of Italy's most delicious bottles.

Barbera d'Alba and Barbera d'Asti are the main DOCG expressions. Barbera is naturally high in acidity and relatively low in tannins, which means it's immediately approachable, food-friendly, and age-capable in ways that feel paradoxical. A simple Barbera is perfect at 2-3 years old, peppery and fresh. Better examples, especially those aged in oak, develop complexity and can age 10-15 years.

The revolution in Barbera came from producers who realized that oak-aging and lower yields could transform this humble grape into something profound. Wines like Elio Altare's Barbera d'Alba La Villa show that Barbera can be as serious as Barolo—rich, concentrated, with fine tannins and aging potential. Most Barbera sells for $15-30, but premium examples cost $40-60 and reward cellaring.

Barbera's high acidity and bright cherry character make it sensational with aged cheeses, cured meats, and rich pasta dishes. Serve at 60°F.

### Dolcetto: The Forgotten Pleasure

Dolcetto means "little sweet one," though the wines are dry. These are soft, immediately approachable reds meant to be drunk young—usually within 3-5 years. Dolcetto d'Alba is the most prestigious DOCG. The wines offer dark cherry, plum, and almond flavors with soft tannins. They're perfect for casual drinking and often cost $12-20, making them excellent value. Serve at 55-60°F, slightly chilled.

### White Piedmont: Gavi and Roero Arneis

Piedmont also makes two excellent whites worth knowing. **Gavi** (from Cortese grapes) is a crisp, minerally white with green apple, lemon, and sometimes a touch of stone fruit. Gavi di Gavi is the most prestigious sub-zone. These wines are meant to be fresh, so drink them within 2-3 years. Serve cold at 45°F. Price range: $15-30.

**Roero Arneis** is a more textured white with stone fruit and almond notes. Arneis was nearly extinct 40 years ago until producers realized it could make serious wine. Modern examples are increasingly excellent. Serve at 48°F. Price range: $18-35.

## Tuscany: Chianti Classico, Brunello, and the Art of Breaking Rules

Tuscany is Italy's most famous wine region, and its reputation rests on two pillars: Chianti Classico, which perfected traditional Sangiovese-based wine, and the Super Tuscans, which asked "what if we didn't have to follow those rules?"

### Chianti Classico: Understanding the Hierarchy

Chianti is often misunderstood. When you see "Chianti" on a label with no further designation, it's from the broad Chianti zone and is usually a simple, approachable red made for early drinking. It may have been your first Italian wine and might have come with a straw basket (fiasco).

**Chianti Classico** is entirely different. It comes from the original, most prestigious heart of Chianti—the zone between Florence and Siena marked by a black rooster (gallo nero) seal on the label. Chianti Classico must be 100% Sangiovese (or up to 15% other approved varieties). The minimum aging is one year, though better producers age significantly longer.

Chianti Classico Riserva requires minimum 24 months aging and must come from a single vintage. Riserva is the signal that you're getting a wine a producer believes in for the long term.

Sangiovese is a tricky grape. It's high in acidity and tannins, often with flavors of cherry, dried herbs, leather, and a mineral edge. In Chianti, it's usually blended with small amounts of other grapes—traditionally softer varieties like Trebbiano (white) were allowed, but modern Chianti often uses softer reds like Merlot or Cabernet. The best Chianti Classico examples show elegant, racy cherry fruit, fine tannins, and serious aging potential. A good Chianti Classico from a quality producer costs $25-50 for a regular bottling, $40-80 for Riserva.

Top producers include Antinori (multiple estates), Fèlsina, Brolio, Fattoria San Fabiano, and Castello d'Ama. Serve at 58-62°F.

### Brunello di Montalcino: Tuscany's Most Serious Red

South of Chianti lies Montalcino, a small medieval hilltop town that produces Brunello—arguably Tuscany's (and Italy's) greatest red wine. Brunello is 100% Sangiovese Grosso, a clone of Sangiovese that thrives in Montalcino's warm, dry southern microclimate. The regulations are strict: minimum 5 years aging (2 years in oak minimum), and every bottle is analyzed and tasted by an official panel before sale. Brunello is DOCG with genuine guarantees.

These are powerful, structured wines that demand patience. A young Brunello (3-5 years old) is often impenetrable—all tannin and concentration. At 10 years, the wine begins to show its true complexity: dark cherry, plum, tobacco, earth, and a mineral tension that keeps the palate alive. The best examples age 20-30+ years, opening further with time.

Brunello's strict aging requirements mean you're tasting a wine the producer aged deliberately. This contrasts with modern regions where producers bottle younger wines and let consumers age them. Brunello has always been aged under professional scrutiny.

The traditional style (still made by many producers) features extended barrel aging, broader tannins, and a rustic character. Modern producers use shorter barrel time and more fruit purity. Both approaches yield excellent wine; the choice reflects philosophy.

Montalcino also produces **Rosso di Montalcino**, a younger brother made from the same grapes but with less aging (minimum 1 year). These are released sooner and are more approachable, usually costing 40-50% less than Brunello. For drinking within 5-10 years, Rosso di Montalcino offers authentic Brunello character without the wait.

Seek out producers like Biondi-Santi (the founding family), Altesino, Poggio Antico, and Casato Prime Donne. Expect to pay $50-100 for standard Brunello, $80-150+ for prestigious names and older vintages. Serve at 62-65°F.

### Vino Nobile di Montepulciano: The Almost-Equal Alternative

Southeast of Montalcino is Montepulciano, which produces Vino Nobile di Montepulciano—a Sangiovese-based wine with DOCG status and aging requirements (2 years minimum, 1 year in oak). The wines are generally slightly lighter and more graceful than Brunello, though aging requirements approach Brunello's structure. Vino Nobile is usually 15-25% cheaper than comparable Brunello, making it an excellent value alternative.

The town also produces Rosso di Montepulciano (younger, lighter), which offers even better value for near-term drinking.

### Super Tuscans and the Bolgheri Renaissance

The Super Tuscan story began in 1971 when Piero Antinori released Tignanello, a wine made from Sangiovese, Cabernet Sauvignon, and Cabernet Franc—breaking Chianti's sacred requirement for nearly all Sangiovese. The wine was brilliant, complex, and cellar-worthy. But it couldn't be called Chianti because it violated the rules. It was released as a simple table wine (VDT), later becoming IGT when that classification was created.

Tignanello's success proved that a producer could break tradition and create something equal or superior to wines that followed it. This triggered a revolution. Throughout the 1970s and 80s, producers throughout Tuscany began experimenting with French varieties. Some created Bordeaux blends (Cabernet, Merlot, Petit Verdot). Others mixed Sangiovese with small amounts of international varieties. The term "Super Tuscan" applied to any non-traditional, high-quality Tuscan red that didn't fit classical DOCG/DOC definitions.

The most famous Super Tuscan zone is Bolgheri, a small area on the coast where producers like Ornellaia and Sassicaia built world-class reputations on Cabernet-based wines. Sassicaia (Cabernet Sauvignon-based) was so prestigious it eventually earned its own DOCG designation in 1994. Ornellaia (a blend of Cabernet, Merlot, and Cabernet Franc) remains IGT by choice, a declaration that the producer values freedom over traditional classification.

These wines are expensive ($80-200+) and age beautifully for 20-30+ years. They compete directly with top Bordeaux and often win blind tastings. For the cellar, they represent some of Italy's finest investment-grade wines.

More modestly priced Super Tuscans ($30-60) can offer excellent value if you appreciate Bordeaux-style wines with Tuscan soul. The category is philosophically important because it shows that Italian wine rules exist to serve producers and consumers, not to constrain them.

### Vernaccia di San Gimignano: Tuscany's White

San Gimignano is a medieval hilltop town famous for its tower-filled skyline. The local white, Vernaccia di San Gimignano (DOCG), is crisp, minerally, and complex—one of Italy's finest whites. The wine shows green apple, citrus, stone fruit, and a distinctive mineral character. It ages better than most Italian whites, improving for 5-7 years. Serve at 46-48°F. Price: $18-35.

### Vin Santo: The Tuscan Dessert Wine

Vin Santo ("Holy Wine") is Tuscany's sweet wine, made from white grapes (usually Trebbiano and Malvasia) dried on racks for months until concentrated. The dried grapes are then fermented slowly in small oak barrels for years, developing rich, oxidative character. The wine ranges from dry to sweet, with flavors of dried fruit, honey, caramel, and nuts. Quality varies enormously; seek out serious producers. Serve at 55°F in small glasses. Price: $20-40 for decent examples.

## Veneto: Where Appassimento Changed Everything

The Veneto region, in northeastern Italy, is where grapes are dried to concentrate flavor and sugar—a technique called appassimento that produces some of the world's most distinctive wines.

### Amarone della Valpolicella: The Legendary Dried-Grape Wine

Amarone is made in Valpolicella, a collection of valleys near Verona. The name comes from "amaro" (bitter)—supposedly because early Amarone was partially fermented, leaving residual sugar and a slightly bitter edge. Modern Amarone is usually fully dry.

What makes Amarone unique is appassimento. After harvest in early October, grapes (primarily Corvina, with additions of Rondinella and Molinara) are carefully selected and spread on bamboo racks or in crates to dry for approximately 120 days. The grapes must lose at least 40% of their water content through evaporation. This concentration process increases sugar and flavor intensity dramatically. When fermentation occurs, the remaining sugars are converted to alcohol, yielding wines of 15-17% ABV—powerful and rich.

The appassimento method wasn't invented for Amarone; it's an ancient technique used across Mediterranean wine regions. But Amarone represents its most successful modern expression. The wines are full-bodied, viscous, with flavors of dried dark fruit, raisin, chocolate, licorice, and often a savory tobacco or dried herb note. They age beautifully for 15-25+ years.

Amarone is expensive, often $40-80 for solid producers, $100-200+ for top names. The production method and long aging requirement justify the price. A bottle of Amarone represents months of careful work and years of barrel aging before release.

The classic producers include Masi, Allegrini, Dal Forno, and Quintarelli. Serve at 65°F in larger glasses—the wine has significant body and alcohol. It pairs beautifully with rich dishes, aged hard cheeses, and actually works with chocolate desserts.

Valpolicella also produces **Valpolicella Ripasso**, a wine made by passing Valpolicella (a simpler red made by regular vinification) over the skins left from Amarone production. This secondary contact with concentrated grape solids adds body and flavor. Ripasso is lighter than Amarone, more affordable ($18-35), and approachable within 5-8 years.

### Soave: The Unexpected Complexity of White Wine

Soave is Italy's most famous white wine, made from Garganega grapes in a zone east of Verona. Many people know Soave as a simple, clean, crisp white—the "easy" Italian wine. That reputation comes from inexpensive versions, which are perfectly fine for casual drinking.

But serious Soave, especially Soave Classico (the original heartland zone), can be complex and age-worthy. The wines show green apple, stone fruit, mineral, and almond. The best examples come from hillside vineyards where lower yields concentrate flavor. A serious Soave from a quality producer like Pieropan or Suavia can age 8-12 years and cost $25-50. These wines have nothing to do with the simple export versions you might remember.

Serve at 46-48°F.

### Prosecco: The Misunderstood Sparkler

Prosecco is made from Glera grapes in the Veneto and is the world's second-best-selling sparkling wine after Champagne. Most Prosecco is made using the Charmat method (tank fermentation), which creates fine bubbles and preserves fresh fruit flavors. It's sweet, off-dry, or dry (depending on residual sugar levels), usually ranges from 10.5-12% ABV, and costs $15-30.

Prosecco is often dismissed by wine snobs because it's affordable, accessible, and popular. This is unfair. Good Prosecco is delicious—fresh, festive, and food-friendly. The category has exploded in quality in recent years, with serious producers like Ruggeri and Bisol pushing boundaries. Seek out Prosecco Superiore DOCG (higher standards than regular Prosecco DOC) for better structure and complexity.

Serve well-chilled at 40-42°F.

## Alto Adige and Friuli: Where Austria Meets Italy

These northeastern regions sit in the Alps, bordering Austria and sharing genetic, cultural, and wine-making traditions with their neighbor. The climate is cooler, ripening is later, and the approach to white wine is more serious than in warmer parts of Italy.

### Alto Adige: The Alpine White Wine Paradise

Alto Adige (also called Südtirol) produces some of Italy's finest and most food-friendly white wines. The region is famous for Pinot Grigio, Gewürztraminer, and increasingly, red wines like Lagrein.

**Pinot Grigio from Alto Adige** is different from most Pinot Grigio you've tasted. The high altitude (vineyards sit 1,500-2,500 feet above sea level) and cool nights create a long growing season. This extended ripeness generates intense, floral aromas—white peach, pear, apple, sometimes honeysuckle—with pronounced minerality and crisp acidity. These are elegant, refined whites that can age 5-8 years. Serious producers charge $20-40 for Pinot Grigio that tastes nothing like the $12 export versions. Seek out producers like Girlan and Alois Lageder.

**Gewürztraminer** (from the town of Tramin) achieves its highest expression in Alto Adige. The grape's natural tendency toward floral, spice, and honeyed flavors shines in this region. Alto Adige Gewürztraminer shows rosewater, honeysuckle, spice, and stone fruit with remarkable delicacy and freshness—quite different from heavy, over-ripe Gewürztraminers from other regions. These wines age surprisingly well, improving for 5-10 years. Price: $20-35. Serve at 48-50°F.

**Lagrein** is Alto Adige's signature red—a dark, structured wine with spice, dark fruit, and earthy character. It's less famous than Nebbiolo but equally impressive in quality. Serve at 60°F.

### Friuli: The Orange Wine Frontier

Friuli, east of Alto Adige, has become famous for orange wines—white wines made with extended skin contact, giving them color and tannin structure. This ancient technique, traditional in Georgia and increasingly popular in natural wine circles, creates textured, complex whites.

Friuli also produces excellent conventional whites. **Friulano** (formerly called Tocai Friulano) is a dry white with stone fruit, herb, and mineral notes. **Ribolla Gialla** is increasingly fashionable, especially as an orange wine, showing unusual texture and aging potential. These wines range from $20-50 for quality examples and represent serious, modern Italian white winemaking.

The Friuli approach is deliberately different from other Italian regions—less about tradition, more about experimentation and expressing place through unusual techniques.

## Southern Italy: The Volcanic Renaissance

Southern Italy—Campania, Basilicata, Calabria, Puglia, and Sicily—was long dismissed as simple wine country. In the last 20 years, the region has experienced a renaissance driven by international attention, investment, and a realization that ancient volcanic soils and traditional grape varieties could produce world-class wines.

### Sicily and Mount Etna: Nerello Mascalese and the Volcanic Story

Mount Etna, Europe's most active volcano, sits on the island of Sicily. Its volcanic slopes have been terraced and planted for millennia. In the last 15-20 years, Etna's wines have become fashionable—particularly those made from native grapes like Nerello Mascalese.

**Nerello Mascalese** is a red grape from Sicily, historically undistinguished, now recognized as one of Italy's great reds. On Etna's volcanic slopes, it produces elegant, mineral-driven wines with flavors of dark cherry, red fruit, spice, and earth. The acidity is high, tannins are fine, and the aging potential is 15-25+ years. These wines are thrilling because they taste completely different from Italian reds you've tasted—more Burgundy-like in elegance and freshness than the broader tannins of Nebbiolo or Sangiovese.

Etna is expensive by Southern Italian standards ($30-80 for quality examples) because demand has outpaced supply. But the wines justify the price. Seek out producers like Girolamo Russo, Frank Cornelissen, and Benanti.

**Carricante** is Etna's white grape, showing minerality, stone fruit, and a distinctive volcanic character. These whites age beautifully and pair excellently with seafood.

### Aglianico: The Barolo of the South

Aglianico is ancient—probably brought to Campania by Greek settlers. It's a powerful, structured red grape that thrives in the volcanic soils of Campania and Basilicata. The wines are often called "the Barolo of the South" because of their age-worthiness and complexity, though they taste completely different.

**Taurasi** (DOCG) is Aglianico's most prestigious expression, made in Campania north of Naples. The wines are powerful, often high in alcohol (14-15%), with flavors of dark plum, cherry, tobacco, earth, and sometimes leather. They require aging—5-10 years minimum to show complexity—and can age 20-30+ years. These are serious, old-fashioned wines.

**Aglianico del Vulture** comes from Basilicata, around Mount Vulture. The wines are similar in structure to Taurasi but often slightly more elegant. Both expressions are worth exploring for a different take on Italian reds.

Expect to pay $25-50 for solid Aglianico, $50-100+ for top producers like Mastroberardino or Feudi di San Gregorio.

### Campania's Whites: Fiano and Greco

Campania produces two excellent native whites worth knowing:

**Fiano di Avellino** (DOCG) shows stone fruit, citrus, honey, and mineral notes with surprising complexity. It ages 5-8 years. Price: $20-35.

**Greco di Tufo** (DOCG) is crisper and more mineral, with citrus and green apple. Price: $18-30.

Both are serious alternatives to the more famous whites of northern Italy.

### Puglia: Primitivo and Negroamaro

Puglia, in the heel of Italy's boot, is hot, dry, and historically known for rustic reds. In recent decades, producers have realized the region's potential for serious wine.

**Primitivo** is genetically identical to California's Zinfandel (though Primitivo's origins are Croatian). In Puglia, it produces rich, dark wines with flavors of ripe plum, fig, and spice, usually between 13.5-16% ABV. Primitivo can be made dry or with residual sugar (in the appassimento style, similar to Amarone). Dry Primitivos are more food-friendly; sweet versions work as dessert wines. Price: $15-35.

**Negroamaro** is Puglia's other red, darker and more austere than Primitivo, often showing licorice, dark fruit, and earth. These wines age well and are increasingly excellent. Price: $15-30.

Both represent excellent value for serious, age-worthy reds from an increasingly important region.

### Sardinia: The Underrated Island

Sardinia (Sardegna) produces distinctive wines from Grenache (called Cannonau locally), Vermentino, and Monica. Cannonau Wines are powerful, often high in alcohol, with earthy, rustic character. Vermentino is a crisp white that works beautifully with seafood. Sardinia remains understated compared to Sicily and the mainland, but prices are lower and quality is often excellent. These represent genuine value wines.

## The Future of Italian Wine

Italian wine is simultaneously ancient and wildly contemporary. The country's willingness to honor tradition while embracing innovation—to respect rules while celebrating those who break them—has created a wine culture of remarkable depth.

Understanding Italy means understanding that a $15 Prosecco and a $150 Brunello are equally valuable in their contexts. It means recognizing that tradition is a starting point, not a straitjacket. It means appreciating that the smallest villages often produce the most distinctive wines, and that the best sommelier's trick is knowing when to recommend the famous wine and when to recommend something obscure from a small producer nobody has heard of.

Italian wine rewards curiosity, regional thinking, and the understanding that the rule-breakers often make the best wines.
