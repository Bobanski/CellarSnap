# Winemaking, Tasting, and Service — A Sommelier's Guide

## Part I: The Winemaking Process

### Understanding Harvest Decisions

The moment a winemaker decides to pick the grapes is one of the most consequential decisions in the entire vintage. This timing determines everything: sugar levels, acidity balance, phenolic ripeness (the maturity of tannins and flavors), and the overall character of the finished wine. A grape picked too early will yield thin, herbaceous wine with harsh acidity and green tannins. Pick too late, and you risk losing freshness, or worse—compromising the balance entirely.

Professional winemakers monitor maturity through three lenses. First, they track Brix (sugar content) using a refractometer. Second, they measure titratable acidity and pH to ensure freshness will be preserved. Third, they taste the seeds and skins to assess tannin ripeness—you want seeds that taste bitter (mature) rather than green (unripe). A Cabernet Sauvignon destined for aging might wait until sugar hits 24–26 Brix and pH climbs to 3.8. A Riesling might be picked at 19 Brix with 7 g/L acidity to preserve that cutting minerality.

Weather plays a huge role. A warm vintage pushes ripeness earlier; a cool year might require patience into late September. In regions like Bordeaux, rain two weeks before the target pick date can force an agonizing decision: harvest now under grape sugar and risk unripe tannins, or wait and risk mold and dilution from moisture. Most years, you lose either way.

### Fermentation: The Heart of Winemaking

Fermentation is where yeast converts sugar into alcohol and carbon dioxide, while simultaneously creating hundreds of flavor compounds. The temperature at which fermentation happens directly affects the flavor profile and the speed of the process. This is where commercial choices matter enormously.

#### Temperature Control

White wines are typically fermented cool, between 45–60°F (7–15°C). This slower fermentation preserves volatile aromatic compounds—the citrus, stone fruit, and floral notes you want in a Sauvignon Blanc or Albariño. Cold fermentation also keeps volatile acidity (the vinegar-like character) from spiking and maintains the wine's freshness. A Chablis fermented at 55°F will taste brighter and more mineral-driven than one fermented at 68°F, where the fruit becomes riper and more tropical.

Red wines ferment warmer, typically 70–85°F (21–30°C). Warmer temperatures extract better color from the skins and help develop ripe, complex tannins. They also speed the process. A Pinot Noir fermented at 75°F might finish in 10–14 days; one at 55°F could drag on for three weeks, risking unwanted oxidation.

Many premium producers use temperature control to their advantage, dropping the temperature in the final days of fermentation to preserve aromatic compounds and stop the yeast before all residual sugar is consumed (for naturally off-dry wines).

#### Yeast Selection: Ambient vs. Commercial

Some winemakers rely on ambient (wild) yeast already present on the grape skins and in the cellar. This is less predictable but can yield complex, site-expressive wines. The fermentation might start slowly, stall unexpectedly, or introduce unwanted flavors. Others inoculate with commercial yeast strains selected for consistency, alcohol tolerance, or specific flavor characteristics.

Common commercial strains are named by their source region. Alsatian yeasts (like EC-1118) ferment reliably and tend to highlight citrus and mineral notes in white wines. Burgundy strains ferment slower and produce more floral, elegant profiles. For reds, strains like Côtes de Beaune or Rhône yeasts can accentuate tannin structure or fruit character depending on the choice.

Modern research shows that certain commercial yeasts now ferment reliably at higher temperatures than traditional strains, improving energy efficiency and reducing environmental impact—a growing concern in premium winemaking.

### Maceration and Skin Contact

For red wines, maceration is the time grapes spend in contact with their skins during and after fermentation. This is where color, tannin, and phenolic compounds transfer into the juice. A short maceration (7 days) yields lighter, more fruit-forward reds. Extended maceration (21+ days) produces darker, more structured wines with grippier tannins. Some premium producers even conduct cold maceration before fermentation begins—soaking the grapes in cold juice for days to extract color without alcohol.

For white wines, brief skin contact (2–8 hours) before pressing can add texture, flavor, and a subtle golden hue without the oxidation risk of longer maceration. Many orange wines (white wines made with extended skin contact) ferment and age with skins for weeks, developing rich, tannin-driven profiles reminiscent of light reds.

### Pressing and Separation

Once fermentation ends, the winemaker presses the remaining solid material (pomace) to extract the remaining liquid. This press wine is darker, more tannic, and more concentrated than the "free-run" juice that drained naturally. Most producers blend some press wine back in for structure but avoid too much, which can overwhelm delicate wines. A light Pinot Noir might include 10–15% press wine; a Barossa Shiraz might go to 40%.

### Malolactic Fermentation: The Secret Softener

After primary fermentation ends, most red wines and some white wines undergo malolactic fermentation (MLF). This is not fermentation by yeast—it's a secondary biological process where lactic acid bacteria (primarily Oenococcus oeni) convert sharp malic acid into softer lactic acid. This is why a Burgundy or Bordeaux tastes rounder and less tart than the raw fermenting wine.

MLF produces diacetyl as a byproduct, which gives wines a creamy, buttery character. Chardonnay owes much of its signature richness to MLF. The process typically takes weeks or months, depending on temperature and the strain of bacteria. Winemakers can inoculate with commercial cultures to ensure it happens reliably or allow it to occur naturally.

Some wines, particularly aromatic whites like Riesling and Sauvignon Blanc, skip MLF entirely to preserve freshness and cut. Sparkling wines typically avoid it as well, because the creamy notes would muddy the crisp, delicate profile expected in Champagne and Cava.

### Aging: Oak, Steel, and Everything In Between

How and where a wine ages dramatically shapes its final character. Oak is the most traditional and debated choice.

#### Oak Types and Toast Levels

French oak is tight-grained, subtle, and complex. It imparts vanilla, spice, and toast notes slowly and elegantly. This is why Burgundies and Bordeaux are aged in French oak—it integrates seamlessly with the wine. French oak barrels cost $900–$2,000 each.

American oak is looser-grained and more aggressive, contributing vanilla, coconut, and bold wood spice. Bourbon barrels (which are made from American oak) are cheaper and impart stronger oak character, which is why they're popular in new-world wines and some Spanish Riojas seeking riper styles. A used American oak barrel might cost $300–$600.

Hungarian oak splits the difference—moderate grain tightness and spice, somewhere between French and American in price and impact.

Toast level matters enormously. Light toast emphasizes wood spice and tannin structure. Medium toast brings vanilla and caramel. Heavy toast contributes smoky, charred notes and smoother texture. A winemaker might use three different toast levels in different lots to build complexity.

#### New Oak vs. Seasoned Oak

New oak (first use) is intense. A wine aged entirely in new oak for 18 months can taste woody and overpowering. Most producers use a mix: perhaps 30% new oak and 70% second- or third-use oak to allow fruit to shine while gaining complexity. Expensive Bordeaux wines might spend 18–24 months in mostly new oak and still balance beautifully because the fruit concentration and structure support the wood. A lighter wine in the same setup would be undrinkable.

Seasoned oak—barrels that have been used before—imparts subtle vanilla and spice without the aggressive tannins of new oak.

#### Alternatives to Oak

Stainless steel tanks maintain fruit freshness and allow very clean flavors to emerge. Most white wines and many light reds are aged in steel. It's inert, temperature-stable, and doesn't impart flavor.

Concrete eggs and amphorae have gained popularity in recent decades for bringing subtle minerality and texture without the aggressiveness of oak. They're porous enough to allow micro-oxidation (gentle contact with oxygen) but neutral in flavor. Wines aged in concrete or amphora often taste fresher and more vibrant than those in oak, which appeals to producers seeking balance over oak sweetness.

Large wood vats or foudres (300+ liter oak vessels) allow slow oxidation and flavor integration without the intense oak tannins of smaller barrels. Burgundies and German wines often use these.

#### Fining and Filtration

Before bottling, most wines are clarified. Fining agents (egg white, bentonite clay, isinglass from fish bladder) bind to tiny particles and settle, dropping them out. Some winemakers use mechanical filtration—running the wine through a pad or membrane to physically remove particles.

Unfined and unfiltered wines are cloudier but retain more subtle flavors and texture. They're fashionable in natural wine circles but can throw sediment over time.

### Sulfur Dioxide: The Essential Preservative

Sulfur dioxide (SO₂) is added at various stages to prevent oxidation and inhibit spoilage organisms. A winemaker might add it at crush, after fermentation, during aging, and before bottling. The amount varies by style: a delicate white wine might receive 40–80 ppm (parts per million), while a full-bodied red might get 50–100 ppm.

Higher-alcohol wines, reds, and long-aging wines require more sulfur because they're at greater risk of oxidation. Young, fresh, high-acid whites need less. All wines contain some sulfur naturally from fermentation—commercial additions simply ensure stability.

Contrary to popular belief, sulfites don't cause wine headaches. Dried apricots contain 5–10 times more sulfites than wine without the same health concerns. Wine headaches typically stem from alcohol, histamines (compounds formed during fermentation), or tannins—not sulfites.

## Part II: Wine Tasting Methodology

### The Systematic Approach: See, Swirl, Smell, Sip, Savor

Professional wine tasting follows a structured method that separates observation from interpretation. This systematic approach reveals a wine's condition, age, origin, and quality.

### Appearance: What the Eye Tells You

Pour the wine into a proper glass (tulip-shaped, 12–15 oz capacity) and tilt it against a white background. You're assessing three things: color, clarity, and intensity.

**Color** indicates grape variety, age, and winemaking choices. A young Cabernet Sauvignon is deep purple; a Pinot Noir is lighter ruby. After 5–10 years, reds fade toward garnet and brown at the edges. White wines deepen from pale yellow to gold to amber as they age. A white wine that's already brown at 5 years old has oxidized too much. Sparkling wines range from pale yellow (Blanc de Blancs) to deep copper (rosé).

**Clarity** shows whether the wine is clean or faulty. Most wines are crystal clear. Haziness or cloudiness might indicate a fault—oxidation, bacterial spoilage, or excess sediment. However, many unfiltered and naturally made wines are intentionally hazy without quality issues.

**Intensity** relates to concentration and alcohol. A wine with deep, saturated color likely comes from ripe grapes and might show higher alcohol. Paler wines are often lighter-bodied or from cooler vintages.

### Nose: Identifying Aromas

Swirl the wine gently to aerate it, then inhale from the glass. The nose (or bouquet) reveals flavor compounds and potential faults.

#### First Nose vs. Air

The first impression, taken immediately after pouring, shows the "first nose"—fresh, volatile fruit aromas that greet you immediately. After a few minutes of aeration and oxidation, secondary and tertiary aromas emerge. Swirling accelerates this process.

#### Aroma Categories and What They Mean

Primary aromas come from the grape variety and fermentation. These are fruit-forward: citrus, stone fruit (peach, apricot), berries, tropical fruit for white wines; red fruit (cherry, plum), dark fruit (blackberry, black cherry) for reds. Primary aromas are what you want to smell in young wines.

Secondary aromas develop during fermentation and aging. In whites, you might detect baked apple, honey, hazelnut, or yeasty/brioche notes—especially in Chardonnay or Champagne that's undergone MLF. In reds, secondary aromas include leather, tobacco, dried herbs, or game notes.

Tertiary aromas emerge during long aging. These are complex: dried fruit, mushroom, forest floor, tar, prune, or tea notes. A 15-year-old Bordeaux will smell of tertiary aromas far more than primary fruit.

Common descriptors decoded:
- **Mineral**: flint, slate, wet stone—indicates cool-climate origin, high acidity, and low ripeness. It's a positive descriptor meaning freshness, not a taste of rocks.
- **Earthy**: wet soil, forest floor, mushroom—common in aged wines and certain terroirs.
- **Spicy**: pepper, clove, cinnamon—from oak or certain grape compounds, not added spices.
- **Floral**: rose, violet, peony—common in Riesling, Gewürztraminer, and Pinot Noir.
- **Buttery/creamy**: from MLF and new oak, not actually butter.
- **Jammy**: overripe fruit, high alcohol, less freshness—can be positive in ripe vintages but suggests lower acidity.

#### Faults and Off-Aromas

A cork-tainted wine smells of wet cardboard or damp basement—this is TCA (2,4,6-trichloroanisole), a mold compound. The wine isn't harmful, but it's faulty and should be returned.

Vinegar or nail polish remover smells suggest volatile acidity (acetic acid or acetaldehyde), indicating oxidation or infection.

Sulfur or rotten egg aromas, called reduction, can occur in wines sealed too tightly. Swirl and let the wine breathe for several minutes—many reductive wines blow off and smell fine again.

### Palate: Assessing Structure and Flavor

Now taste. Slurp a small amount, drawing air through your mouth (yes, it's intentional and correct). This aerates the wine and helps volatile compounds reach your olfactory bulb, where you actually perceive most taste.

Assess five key dimensions:

**Sweetness**: Even a dry wine contains a few grams of residual sugar. You're noting whether the wine is bone-dry (less than 1 g/L), dry (1–5 g/L), off-dry (5–15 g/L), or sweet (15+ g/L). This is detected on the tip of the tongue.

**Acidity**: The tartness or freshness. High acidity feels crisp and refreshing; low acidity tastes flabby and dull. This is detected on the sides of the tongue and roof of the mouth. A wine with good acidity should feel lively and balanced, not puckering.

**Tannin**: The drying sensation in red wines, like biting the inside of a grape skin. Tannins feel gravelly or astringent. Young wines have harsh tannins; aged wines have softened, integrated tannins. Tannins are a framework for aging—more tannin means more potential longevity.

**Body**: The weight or thickness in your mouth. This correlates to alcohol and extract (solids like tannins, glycerin, and phenolics). A light-bodied wine might be a Pinot Noir at 12.5% alcohol; a full-bodied wine is a Shiraz at 15%. Body isn't quality—elegant reds can be light-bodied.

**Alcohol**: Warmth at the back of your throat. Wines range from 8–9% (some Rieslings) to 17% (some fortified or late-harvest wines). At 14–15%, it's present but balanced in most wines.

#### Flavor vs. Aroma

The flavors you perceive while tasting largely come from smell (retronasal olfaction—aromas traveling from the back of your mouth to your nose). The five tastes your tongue actually detects are: sweet, salty, sour, bitter, and umami. Most of what we call "taste" is smell. This is why food tastes boring when you have a cold.

Look for fruit flavors that match the nose, plus palate-specific notes: ripeness, balance, finish (the lingering taste after you swallow), and the overall harmony of the five structural dimensions. A wine might have beautiful fruit but be unbalanced if acidity is too low or tannins too high.

### Conclusion: Putting It Together

Summarize the wine's quality, age, origin (if possible), and drinking window. Is this wine balanced and well-made? Is it past its prime or still improving? Should it be consumed now or cellared further? A great tasting note captures both objective observations (alcohol 14.5%, medium ruby color, high acidity) and subjective impressions (elegant, food-friendly, drinking beautifully).

## Part III: Service and Storage

### Serving Temperature: The 45-Minute Rule

Temperature is non-negotiable. Wine served too warm tastes loose and alcoholic; too cold tastes muted and astringent.

**Red wines**: Serve between 55–65°F (13–18°C). Light reds (Pinot Noir, Beaujolais) are best at 55–60°F. Medium reds (Grenache, Barbera) at 60–63°F. Full-bodied reds (Cabernet Sauvignon, Shiraz) at 63–65°F. Room temperature (68–72°F) is too warm—it masks freshness and emphasizes alcohol. A bottle stored in a cellar at 55°F and brought to the table needs no extra cooling. But a bottle from a warm restaurant or home needs 15–20 minutes in a cool place or 5–10 minutes in an ice bucket to reach proper service temperature.

**White wines**: Serve between 45–55°F (7–13°C). Lighter whites (Sauvignon Blanc, Pinot Grigio) at 45–50°F. Fuller-bodied whites (Chardonnay, Albariño) at 50–55°F. Sweeter whites (Riesling, Sauternes) can go slightly warmer, 48–55°F, to express flavor. Putting a white wine in the freezer for 10 minutes or ice bucket for 15–20 minutes gets it to temperature without shocking it.

**Sparkling wines**: Serve between 43–47°F (6–8°C)—nearly ice cold. Champagne and Prosecco get an ice bucket for 20–30 minutes before service.

#### The 45-Minute Rule

Here's a practical principle: when you open a wine, it has roughly 45 minutes before its temperature begins to drift significantly away from serving temperature due to the ambient air (in a room at 70°F). A red at perfect 62°F will creep toward 65°F and warmer as the hour goes on. In winter or a cool restaurant, drift is slower. In summer or a warm room, faster. This is why glass shape matters—wider glasses lose heat faster than narrow ones.

### Decanting: When and Why

Decanting serves two purposes: it separates sediment from wine, and it allows the wine to breathe (aerate).

**Sediment removal**: Aged red wines (10+ years) often develop sediment—the remnants of dead yeast, tannins, and pigments that precipitate over time. To decant, open the bottle upright the evening before serving, then slowly pour into a decanter, watching the bottle's neck as light from a candle or phone flashlight shines through. When sediment appears in the neck, stop pouring. Decant gently; aggressive pouring oxidizes the wine too quickly.

**Aeration**: Young, tannic reds benefit from 30–60 minutes of air exposure. Decanting accelerates the softening of rough tannins and lets volatile compounds volatilize. Pour the wine into a decanter (or any clean pitcher), let it sit, then serve. Older wines (15+ years) should be decanted closer to service because they're fragile—too much air exposure and the wine becomes flat. Some bottles need only 10–15 minutes.

White wines and lighter reds rarely need decanting unless they have sediment. Sparkling wines should never be decanted—you'll lose the bubbles.

### Glassware: The Basics

Proper glassware shapes direct wine to the right part of your palate and concentrate aromas. A large bowl captures aromas and allows swirling; a narrower rim directs wine to your mouth efficiently.

**All-purpose glass**: The ISO/IEC tasting glass or a standard wine glass (12–15 oz tulip shape) works for nearly everything. It's elegant, versatile, and concentrates aromas without being overly specialized.

**Bordeaux glass**: Larger bowl, taller, narrower at the rim. Best for bold reds with high tannin. The shape directs wine toward the middle of your mouth, past the tannin-sensitive back of your throat.

**Burgundy glass**: Wider bowl, shorter, more rounded. Designed for Pinot Noir and other delicate reds; the wider bowl lets you appreciate subtle aromas without the aggressive grip of tannins.

**White wine glass**: Smaller bowl, narrower rim, sometimes a stem. Keeps white wine cooler and directs it toward the tip of your tongue (where sweetness is perceived) before the back of your mouth.

**Champagne flute**: Tall, narrow bowl. Preserves bubbles and directs them upward, creating a visual spectacle. Coupe glasses (shallow bowls) are traditional but allow bubbles to escape faster.

The stem matters: it lets you hold the glass without warming the wine with your hand. Never cup the bowl with your palm while drinking.

### Storage Conditions: Long-Term Preservation

Wine ages gracefully only if stored properly.

**Temperature**: 45–65°F (7–18°C) is ideal, with 55°F being close to perfect. Consistency matters more than absolute temperature—wild swings between hot and cold age wine prematurely. A basement or wine refrigerator maintains this better than a kitchen cabinet.

**Position**: Bottles with cork should be stored horizontally (lying flat). This keeps the cork moist, preventing it from drying out and letting oxygen in. Screw-cap bottles and sparkling wines can be stored upright.

**Light**: UV light degrades wine, especially whites and sparkling. Dark glass provides some protection, but bottles should live in the dark—cellars, closets, or wine fridges.

**Humidity**: 50–80% is ideal. Too dry and corks shrink; too wet and labels deteriorate. Most cellars naturally maintain this range.

**Stillness**: Vibration and constant movement stress wine. A dedicated wine fridge or cellar is better than a kitchen counter next to a refrigerator that cycles on and off.

Wines meant for young drinking (most whites, rosés, and lighter reds) should be consumed within 1–3 years and stored in cool, dark conditions but don't demand the precision of a fine cellar.

## Part IV: Wine Aging and Drinking Windows

### What Makes a Wine Age-Worthy

Only about 3–5% of wine produced globally is meant for extended cellaring. Most wine is made to be enjoyed within a few years. To age gracefully, a wine needs specific structural components.

**Acidity** acts as a natural preservative, especially in white wines. High acidity keeps the wine fresh and balanced as it ages. A Riesling with 8 g/L titratable acidity will age better than one with 4 g/L, all else equal.

**Tannin** provides structure in red wines. Tannins polymerize (link together) over years, becoming smoother and more integrated. A wine with barely any tannin will taste watery at 10 years; a wine with adequate tannin will have evolved beautifully, with the tannins now soft and silky.

**Concentration** means the wine has enough fruit and flavor compounds to sustain aging. If a wine barely has enough fruit at release, those flavors will fade before the structure softens. A concentrated wine can lose some fruit intensity and still taste complex. Think of it as a bank account: you need enough balance to withdraw from for years.

**Alcohol** provides both preservation and structure, but too much (over 16%) can unbalance the wine and cause it to taste hot or flat over time.

A wine that checks all four boxes—high acidity, good tannin, concentration, and balanced alcohol—can age for 20–50+ years. Bordeaux, Burgundy, Barossa Shiraz, Northern Rhône reds, and premium white Burgundy excel here. A fresh, light Pinot Grigio with low acidity and delicate fruit will never age beautifully no matter how carefully you store it.

### Typical Drinking Windows by Category

**Everyday red wines** (lighter Pinots, Barberas, Grenaches): Drink within 3–5 years of release. They fade after that.

**Standard Cabernet Sauvignons and Bordeaux blends**: Peak from 5–15 years after vintage. A 2015 Napa Cabernet is drinking well now (2026) and will for another decade.

**Premium Bordeaux (First Growths, serious châteaux)**: 10–30 years after vintage. A 2015 Château Latour is still infant at 2026; it will peak around 2035–2040.

**Burgundy (Pinot Noir)**: 5–20 years depending on the producer. Village-level bottles drink in 5–8 years; Premier Cru and Grand Cru go 15–25.

**Riojas (aged in barrel before release)**: The Gran Reserva category spends 2+ years in barrel, so it's already aged. Drink now or hold 10–15 years further.

**White Burgundy and high-end white Bordeaux blends**: 8–25 years. These improve steadily for a decade and hold at peak for another 5–15.

**Riesling**: Can age 10–40+ years depending on style. Dry Rieslings from Alsace or the Rhine Valley develop incredible complexity. Sweet ones (Auslese, TBA) improve for decades.

**Champagne**: Non-vintage (NV) is ready to drink now; vintage Champagne improves for 10–20 years after release. A 2012 Champagne is dancing now; a 2008 is still evolving.

**Everyday white wines** (Sauvignon Blanc, Pinot Grigio, Albariño): Drink within 1–2 years. Freshness is the point.

### Recognizing a Wine Past Its Prime

When does a wine decline?

**Visual cues**: Oxidized color (brown tones in reds; deep gold or amber in whites at 3–5 years), sediment that's turned cloudy rather than settled, or a cork that's leaking all signal trouble.

**Nose**: Vinegar, nail polish remover, or flat, dull aromas instead of fruit indicate oxidation or age-related degradation.

**Palate**: The wine tastes flat, watery, or thin. Tannins haven't softened—they've vanished, leaving nothing. Fruit has faded entirely, and no interesting secondary flavors emerged. Acidity tastes sharp or out of balance because alcohol has evaporated slightly.

Some wines decline gracefully; others hit a wall. A great wine might improve steadily until year 20, maintain a plateau for another 10, then decline over the next few years. A lesser wine might peak at year 6 and start declining immediately after.

### Storage Faults vs. Age-Related Decline

Not all deterioration is natural aging. A wine stored in a hot garage ages prematurely and tastes cooked, not complex. One stored upright with a cork for decades might be corked (TCA-tainted) or oxidized, not noble in age. The difference is how the wine tastes: noble aging adds layers of complexity and softens harsh edges; storage faults taste off, vinegary, or flat.

## Part V: Common Misconceptions Debunked

### "Screw Caps Are Only for Cheap Wine"

This myth persists despite overwhelming evidence to the contrary. Screw caps provide a more consistent seal than cork, preventing cork taint (TCA contamination) and oxidation. They're ideal for wines meant to drink fresh and bright: Sauvignon Blancs, Rieslings, and many New Zealand and Australian wines, including premium bottles worth $50+. The New Zealand wine industry adopted screw caps en masse for quality control reasons, and their wines are world-class.

Cork is traditional and adds ritual to opening a bottle, but screw caps are objectively better at preserving freshness for young wines. If a wine is faulty, it's more likely from cork taint than screw cap failure. Use cork for wines you're cellaring long-term; screw cap for everyday drinking and fresh whites.

### "Sulfites Cause Wine Headaches"

Sulfites get blamed for hangovers and allergies, but the science doesn't support it. Wine contains 20–300 ppm sulfites depending on style; dried apricots contain 1,000–3,000 ppm. If sulfites caused headaches, dried fruit would wreak havoc on anyone sensitive. Wine headaches stem from alcohol (which causes dehydration), histamines (compounds from fermentation that some people are sensitive to), or tannins—not sulfites. All-natural wines with zero added sulfites still contain residual sulfites from fermentation.

### "The 'Legs' on a Glass Mean the Wine Is Good"

The streaks running down the inside of a glass after you swirl it are called legs or tears. People assume prominent legs indicate quality, higher alcohol, or glycerin content. While legs do correlate somewhat with alcohol and viscosity, they're not a quality indicator. A simple wine with high alcohol might have beautiful legs; a complex, lower-alcohol wine might have minimal legs. Ignore them and focus on nose, palate, and balance.

### "Higher Price Always Means Better Wine"

Price correlates with quality up to a point, but it plateaus quickly. A $30 wine is usually better than a $10 wine. A $100 wine might be only marginally better—the extra cost reflects rarity, prestige, or provenance, not incremental quality. A 2015 Château Latour ($400) is exceptional, but a 2015 Château Giscours ($50) is genuinely excellent. Personal preference and cellar conditions matter more than price. A wine you love is better than an expensive wine you merely admire.

### "All Rosé Is Sweet"

Rosé ranges from bone-dry to dessert-level sweet. Most serious rosés from Provence, southern France, and Spain are dry or off-dry with crisp acidity and subtle strawberry or citrus flavors. They're not summer girlie-drinks—they're food-friendly and structured. American rosés and mass-produced versions tend toward sweetness, which is where the stereotype comes from. Taste before assuming, and look for dry rosés from known regions for the best quality.

### "Natural Wine Is Healthier"

Natural wine is made with minimal intervention—no added sulfites (or minimal), no commercial yeast, no fining agents. The philosophy is admirable, but "natural" doesn't equal "healthier." Many natural wines are delicious and complex; others are faulty (infected, oxidized, volatile) because minimal sulfites leave them vulnerable to spoilage. The health claim is marketing. Drink natural wine if you enjoy the taste and philosophy, not because it's nutritionally superior to conventional wine.

### "Older Wine Is Always Better"

Age is not a goal. A wine reaches peak quality when its structure and flavor are most harmonious, then declines. For many wines, this peak comes within 1–5 years. Cellaring a wine past its prime is a waste. Know the drinking window for your wine—buy with intention and drink with purpose.

## Tasting and Service: Practical Takeaways

To taste wine professionally, follow the five-step approach: observe the appearance, assess the nose before and after aeration, then evaluate the palate across sweetness, acidity, tannin, body, and alcohol. Finish with a conclusion about quality and drinking timeline.

To serve properly, hit the target temperature (55–65°F for reds, 45–55°F for whites, 43–47°F for sparkling), use proper glassware, and remember the 45-minute rule for temperature drift. Store long-term bottles horizontally in the dark at steady, cool conditions.

Understand that winemaking is a series of choices—harvest timing, fermentation temperature, oak selection, malolactic fermentation, fining, and sulfite additions—each shaping the final wine. A sommelier who understands these choices understands why wines taste the way they do and can communicate that knowledge to guests.

The misconceptions that linger—about screw caps, sulfites, and price—persist because they're comforting stories, not evidence. The more you taste, serve, and learn, the easier it becomes to separate myth from reality. Trust your palate, taste widely, and build your knowledge bottle by bottle.
