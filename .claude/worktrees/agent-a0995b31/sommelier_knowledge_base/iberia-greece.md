# The Wines of Spain, Portugal, and Greece: A Sommelier's Guide

These three Mediterranean wine countries represent some of the world's most distinctive terroirs, indigenous grapes, and winemaking traditions. From the oak-aged reds of northern Spain to the volcanic whites of Greece, Iberia and the Greek islands offer remarkable value, complexity, and food-friendliness. Understanding their regional distinctions and aging systems is essential for any wine professional.

---

## SPAIN: The World's Most Diverse Wine Country

Spain produces more wine than any other country on Earth, yet most wine professionals know only Rioja and Ribera del Duero. The reality is far richer: Spain has 146 designated wine regions, each with distinct terroir, traditions, and character. What unites them is Spain's commitment to regionality, indigenous grapes, and serious aging potential.

### Rioja: Spain's Most Traditional Region

Rioja holds Spain's highest classification: DOCa (Denominación de Origen Calificada), the only other region with this rank being Priorat. With over 600 wineries, Rioja is synonymous with the Spanish wine tradition—and for good reason. The region's fame rests on Tempranillo, a grape that thrives in Rioja's sheltered river valley where Atlantic and Mediterranean climates collide.

**Rioja's Three Subregions:** Rioja Alavesa (north of the Ebro River) tends toward lighter, more elegant wines with earthier characteristics. Rioja Alta, upstream in the cooler foothills, produces medium-bodied wines with more structure and aging potential. Rioja Baja, to the east, is warmer and yields riper, more fruit-forward wines. Understanding these distinctions helps predict style and evolution.

**The Crianza, Reserva, Gran Reserva System:** This is Rioja's greatest gift to understanding Spanish wine. These terms define mandatory oak aging and bottle age, creating a legal framework for development. Crianza must spend minimum one year in oak and two years total aging (one bottle). Wines at this level are approachable at release, showing oak spice and toasted nuts alongside red fruit. Reserva demands minimum two years in oak and three years total aging, producing wines with greater complexity, tertiary character, and aging potential—typically 10 to 20 years. Gran Reserva, the most prestigious, requires two years in oak and five years total aging, creating profound, age-worthy bottles that can outlive decades in the cellar. A Rioja Gran Reserva from a stellar vintage (2004, 2005, 2009, 2011, 2015, 2018) represents one of wine's great values and can age 30+ years.

Iconic producers include Marqués de Murrieta, Marqués de Riscal, La Rioja Alta, Bodegas Ysios, and Viña Tondonia. For value, Rodriguez Palagano and Bodegas Bretón deliver serious quality at modest prices.

**Serving Rioja:** Crianza benefits from one hour of decanting before serving at 60-62°F. Reserva and Gran Reserva improve dramatically with 90 minutes to two hours of air, served at 62-65°F. These wines pair beautifully with roasted lamb, aged Manchego cheese, and slow-braised meats.

### Ribera del Duero: Power and Structure

If Rioja represents tradition, Ribera del Duero embodies modern Spanish power. Located north of Madrid along the Duero River, this region's high altitude (2,400-2,800 feet) and continental climate create Tempranillo wines of remarkable concentration, dark fruit, and structured tannins.

Why is altitude so important here? High elevation extends the growing season, allowing Tempranillo to achieve full physiological ripeness while maintaining freshness and acidity. The result: wines of deeper color, more saturated fruit, and firmer structure than lower-altitude Rioja. Ribera bottles age splendidly, often outpacing their Rioja counterparts.

The region's finest wines come from historic towns like Pesquera, Roa, and Lerma. Bodega Pesquera practically defined the region's modern identity, with densely concentrated wines aging 40+ years. Other benchmark producers—Vega Sicilia, Pingus, Emilio Moro, Alejandro Fernández—command serious prices but deliver ageworthy depth. For value-conscious buyers, look to Bodegas Áster, Bodegas Tempranillo, and Campo Eliseo, which offer Ribera's power at Rioja prices.

Ribera Del Duero wines benefit from three to five years of aging before drinking; a 2015 or 2016 showing excellent structure today will likely hit peak complexity in 2028-2030. Serve at 64-66°F to allow tannins to show elegantly.

### Priorat: The Other DOCa—Slate and Power

Priorat earned its DOCa status in 2000, the first region to receive it since Rioja. This achievement reflects the sheer intensity and uniqueness of Priorat's terroir. Nestled in Catalonia's rocky foothills, Priorat vineyards cascade down steep, terraced hillsides planted on llicorella—a distinctive dark, fissured slate soil that contributes profound minerality and structure.

**Terroir as Teacher:** If you want to understand how soil shapes wine, study Priorat. The llicorella reflects heat back onto vines, concentrating sugars and flavors. Simultaneously, it drains rapidly, forcing roots to dig deep for water and minerals. The result: wines of extraordinary intensity, dark color, and mineral precision. A great Priorat Garnacha doesn't taste like Garnacha from warmer, flatter regions—it tastes like that specific mountain, that specific slate.

Priorat's principal grape is Garnacha, often blended with Carignan and Syrah. Wines typically carry 14.5-15.5% alcohol and require serious food pairing—slow-roasted lamb, aged Manchego, wild boar, mushroom risotto. The region's leading names include Alvaro Palacios (whose l'Ermita commands auction prices), Clos Mogador, Clos Dofi, and Laurel. For entry-level Priorat, Gratavinum and Clos de l'Obac deliver authenticity at accessible prices.

Expect to age quality Priorat 5-10 years; a 2015 from a top producer is just entering its window now.

### Rías Baixas: Spain's Best White Wine (and Few Drink It Outside Spain)

Rías Baixas, in northwestern Galicia, produces Albariño—a white of such clarity, minerality, and food-friendliness that it's arguably Spain's most versatile wine. Yet outside Iberia, Albariño remains relatively unknown compared to Portuguese Vinho Verde or Italian Vermentino. This is wine drinker's luck.

**Albariño's Magic:** Albariño is grown nowhere else with such distinction. Rías Baixas' Atlantic-facing valleys, cool maritime influence, and granitic soils create whites of laser-like acidity (12.5-13.5% alcohol), bracing minerality, and citrus, stone fruit, and sometimes tropical character. The grape is thick-skinned, ripening late, allowing full flavor development while maintaining freshness.

The region comprises five sub-zones, each expressing different facets. Val do Salnés, closest to the ocean, produces the crispest, most mineral Albariños—nearly saline, with melon and lemon zest. Cambados, further inland, yields slightly rounder wines with white peach and apricot notes. Condado do Tea shows more earthiness and herbal undertones. O Rosal, touching the Portuguese border, produces Albariños with more body and tropical fruit character. These distinctions matter: a Val do Salnés Albariño with grilled fish is one wine; the same label from Condado do Tea pairs differently.

**Optimal Drinking:** Albariño is at its best within two to four years of harvest. Drink it cold (45-50°F) but not ice-cold—temperatures below 40°F suppress minerality and aromatic complexity. Classic pairings: grilled seafood, oysters, clams, grilled fish, scallops, and ceviche. Rías Baixas Albariño is also one of wine's great party wines; chill, pour, enjoy.

Top producers: Martín Códax, Pazo de Señoans, Pereira d'Ayala, and Bodegas Do Ferreiro. The 2023 and 2024 vintages are fresh and vibrant.

### Jerez (Sherry): The World's Most Misunderstood Wine Category

Sherry—fortified wine from Jerez de la Frontera in southern Andalucía—has suffered centuries of marketing mistakes. Many drinkers think it's sweet, heavy, or old-fashioned. In truth, Spain's finest Sherries rank among the world's greatest wines: complex, elegant, bone-dry, and capable of stunning food pairing.

**Understanding Sherry Style:** Sherry's complexity stems from a unique production method called the solera system, where wines age in stacked barrels, with older wine continuously blended into younger, creating a living, evolving product. Additionally, Sherry's flavor profile depends partly on whether it develops flor—a native yeast that blankets the wine, creating oxidative or biological aging. Understanding this distinction unlocks Sherry.

**Fino and Manzanilla:** These are Sherry's dry whites, aged under flor, which protects the wine from excessive oxidation while adding yeasty, almond, and salinity complexity. Fino typically carries 15-15.5% alcohol and shows pale gold color with piercing acidity and briny minerality. Manzanilla, produced specifically in the cooler coastal town of Sanlúcar, is slightly lighter and more delicate. Both are bone-dry and meant for immediate consumption (within a year of opening, ideally). Serve at 50-55°F as an aperitif or with Spanish cheese, jamón serrano, anchovies, or gazpacho.

**Amontillado:** As Fino ages, the flor eventually dies (usually after 5-8 years), exposing the wine to oxidation. This transformation—breaking the flor—creates Amontillado: a deeper amber wine with nutty, caramel, and dried fruit complexity. Amontillado carries 16-17% alcohol and shows remarkable complexity: almond, hazelnut, toffee, dried apricot. Unlike Fino, Amontillado improves for years in the glass and ages beautifully in the cellar (10+ years). Serve at 55-60°F with roasted almonds, aged cheese, or light meat dishes.

**Oloroso:** These rich, dark Sherries never develop flor, aging entirely through oxidation. Oloroso shows deep mahogany color, high glycerin, and flavors of walnut, dried fruit, caramel, and chocolate. Most are labeled as sweet (dulce), but dry Olorosos exist and are extraordinary. Oloroso carries 18-20% alcohol. Serve at 60-65°F, either as an after-dinner digestif or with blue cheese and nuts. A top Oloroso from a producer like González Byass or Valdespino rivals Port in complexity and aging potential.

**Palo Cortado:** This is Sherry's wildcard—technically rare, produced when a Fino's flor dies partially and unpredictably. Palo Cortado splits the difference between Amontillado and Oloroso: drier than Oloroso, fuller than Amontillado, with remarkable elegance and complexity. If you want to impress, order a Palo Cortado.

**Pedro Ximénez (PX):** This sweet Sherry made from sun-dried Pedro Ximénez grapes is liquid raisin cake—syrupy, dark, with flavors of molasses, fig, and prune. Serve as a dessert wine or with chocolate, blue cheese, or after-dinner meditation. PX is transformative poured over vanilla ice cream.

**The Common Misconception:** Many assume all Sherry is sweet. In reality, Fino, Manzanilla, Amontillado, and dry Olorosos are bone-dry, complex, and food-friendly. The sweetness comes only from late-harvest additions and specific styles.

### Cava: Spain's Sparkling Alternative (And Why It Matters)

Cava, produced in the Penedès region of Catalonia using the traditional méthode champenoise, represents extraordinary value in sparkling wine. About 95% of Cava production occurs in Penedès, centered around Sant Sadurní d'Anoia, Europe's epicenter of sparkling wine production.

**Why Cava Works:** Unlike Champagne, Cava relies on indigenous Spanish grapes: Macabeo (crisp, floral), Parellada (delicate, citrus), and Xarel-lo (herbal, minerality). These varieties, grown at lower altitude and higher ripeness than Champagne's cool-climate counterparts, produce wines that are naturally floral, fruity, and less mineral—but incredibly food-friendly and satisfying. Production costs are lower, allowing producers to invest in quality fruit and aging without passing Champagne-level markups to consumers.

Top-tier Cavas (Brut Nature, Extra Brut categories, or from producers like Gramona, Recaredo, or Juvé y Camps) rival entry-level Champagne in complexity and age-worthiness. A Gramona III Lustros (five-year aged Cava) offers white fruit, brioche, minerality, and texture that many $35-40 Champagnes cannot match. Serve at 40-45°F.

### Other Spanish Regions Worth Understanding

**Penedès:** Beyond Cava, Penedès produces still wines—often excellent Cabernet Sauvignon, Merlot, and local varieties like Sumoll. Look for producers like Jean Leon (historic pioneer) and Bodega Montserrat.

**Bierzo:** In northwestern Castilla y León, near Portugal, Bierzo specializes in Mencía—a red grape that produces wines of surprising elegance: medium body, bright acidity, spicy mineral character, and earthy undertones. Steep slate hillsides and Atlantic influence create wines that age 8-12 years beautifully. Producers like Descendientes de J. Palacios and Pittacum deliver remarkable quality. A Bierzo Mencía offers excellent value: complex, food-friendly, distinctive—and few wine drinkers know the region.

**Toro:** Along the Duero River, Toro produces massive, tannic reds from Tinta de Toro (local Tempranillo clone). High altitude and warm conditions create dark, powerful wines with alcohol levels of 14.5-15.5%. These are not delicate: pair with slow-roasted lamb, wild boar, intense hard cheeses. A decade of aging mellows the tannins considerably. Bodega Numanthia is the region's standard-bearer.

**Jumilla:** In southeastern Spain, Jumilla is the world's capital of Monastrell, a grape that thrives in hot, dry conditions. Monastrell comprises 85% of plantings and produces deeply colored, ripe, sometimes rustic wines with dark fruit and savory earthiness. Jumilla's greatest asset is value: serious, age-worthy reds at $12-25. The region famously escaped phylloxera in the 19th century, meaning some vines are pre-phylloxera—rare anywhere in Europe. Producers like Bodega Azpilicueta and Sierra de Jumilla deliver authentic regional character.

## PORTUGAL: The Underestimated Giant

Portugal owns one of the world's great wine heritages—250 indigenous grape varieties, many found nowhere else on Earth. Yet international awareness lags far behind Spain or France. For wine professionals, this represents both opportunity and obligation: to understand these wines is to serve your clients better and uncover remarkable value.

### The Douro Valley: Port, Yes, But Dry Wine First

Most wine professionals fixate on Port, forgetting that the Douro Valley produces extraordinary dry red wines increasingly rivaling Bordeaux and northern Rhône in quality. The valley itself is UNESCO-listed: terraced hillsides descending to the Douro River, one of Europe's steepest, most dramatic vineyard landscapes.

**Indigenous Greatness:** The Douro's traditional red varieties—Touriga Nacional, Touriga Franca, Tinta Roriz, Tinta Barroca, Tinto Cão—have evolved over centuries to thrive in this harsh environment. Touriga Nacional is the region's finest: small berries, thick skins, deep color, high tannins, and remarkable aging potential. It's Portugal's answer to Cabernet or Nebbiolo. Tinta Roriz (Tempranillo's Portuguese cousin) adds freshness and fragrance. Tinta Barroca contributes softness and alcohol. The greatest Douro reds blend these varieties, creating wines of astonishing complexity and depth.

**Dry Douro Reds vs. Port:** This distinction matters. Dry Douro reds (labeled Douro DOC) are table wines, typically 13.5-14.5% alcohol, with freshness and elegance. They're world-class and age 15-25 years. Port, by contrast, is fortified (19-20% alcohol) and deliberately oxidized, serving different roles. A food pairing dinner calls for dry Douro; a serious meditation or cheese course welcomes Tawny or Vintage Port. Producers like Quinta do Vale Meão, Quinta de Vargellas, Niepoort, and Dirk Niepoort (who revolutionized the region) craft stunning dry Douro reds. Quinta do Crasto offers remarkable quality at reasonable prices.

**Port: A Brief Overview:** Port's complexity deserves its own section, but the essentials: Vintage Port (declared in exceptional years only, aged 2 years in barrel then 10+ years in bottle) represents the category's apex—dark, powerful, demanding 10-20 years in the cellar to mature. Tawny Port (aged in barrel, developing oxidative character and brown color) offers immediate approachability and costs less; a 10-Year Tawny or 20-Year Tawny is a serious wine. Ruby Port is lighter, fruitier, meant for immediate drinking.

**Food Pairing Wisdom:** Dry Douro reds pair with roasted lamb, game, aged cheeses, and grilled meats. Tawny Port pairs with blue cheese, nuts, caramel desserts. Vintage Port stands alone or with dark chocolate.

### Vinho Verde: Yes, It's Serious Wine

The irony: Vinho Verde (literally "green wine") is Portugal's most exported wine and almost universally misunderstood. Most drinkers encounter mass-market, slightly sweet versions—often under 10% alcohol—and assume the category is simple. The truth is far more interesting.

**What "Green" Really Means:** "Green" refers to the wine's youth and freshness, not color. Traditional Vinho Verde is made from grapes harvested early, at higher acidity and lower alcohol (9-11%), creating zippy, mineral, often slightly fizzy whites. The region's cool Atlantic maritime climate and old traditions of co-fermentation (white and red grapes together) create distinctive character.

**Indigenous Varieties and Real Quality:** The region's diverse indigenous whites—Alvarinho, Trajadura, Loureiro, Arinto, Avesso, and others—each contribute distinct character. Alvarinho-based Vinhos Verde from the Val do Minho subregion (closest to Rías Baixas, just across the Portuguese border) show remarkable complexity: citrus, stone fruit, minerality, and bracing acidity reminiscent of Rías Baixas Albariño, but typically lighter and more delicate. Trajadura adds floral, honeyed notes. Loureiro contributes aromatic, herbal complexity.

**How to Drink It:** The best Vinho Verdes—from producers like Quinta de Aveleda, Quinta do Feital, or Anselmo Mendes—are bone-dry, mineral, food-friendly, and improve for 3-4 years. Chill to 45-48°F and pair with fresh seafood, salads, goat cheese, or lighter poultry dishes. These are summer wines that also work beautifully as aperitifs.

### Dão: The Mountain's Red Treasure

In central Portugal, Dão produces structured, elegant reds that age beautifully yet remain underappreciated internationally. The region's high altitude (1,000-1,600 feet) and granite-based soils create wines of remarkable freshness and minerality—more elegant than Douro's power, more serious than Alentejo's fruit.

**Indigenous Identity:** Dão traditionally relies on red grapes like Jaen (Mencía), Touriga Nacional, Tinta Roriz, Alfrocheiro, and Trincadeira. These varieties thrive in the cool highlands, developing fine tannins and bright acidity. Dão reds have an almost Burgundian elegance—medium body, complex aromatics, mineral precision. They're gastronomic wines, meaning they shine with food rather than standalone meditation.

Producers like Quinta dos Murças and Quinta do Crovelho craft Dão reds worthy of 15-20 year aging. A Dão from a stellar vintage (2009, 2011, 2015, 2017) improves dramatically from age 7-15 years.

**Pairing Insight:** Dão pairs beautifully with wild mushroom risotto, roasted duck, game birds, aged Manchego, or charcuterie. The wines' acidity cuts through rich, savory dishes elegantly.

### Alentejo: Portugal's Fruit-Forward Giant

In southern Portugal, Alentejo is warm, generous, fruit-forward—the antithesis of Dão's elegance or Douro's power. Yet this is where Portugal shows its modern evolution: international-style wines, higher alcohol, ripe fruit, approachable tannins. For many wine drinkers, Alentejo is entry-point Portuguese wine.

**Grape Diversity:** Alentejo reds typically blend Aragonez (Tempranillo), Trincadeira, and increasingly Alicante Bouschet, producing wines with dark fruit (plum, blackberry), spice (black pepper, licorice), and soft tannins. Alcohol often reaches 14-14.5%. Whites from Antão Vaz and Arinto offer tropical fruit and ripeness while maintaining freshness.

These aren't serious agers like Douro or Dão; most Alentejo reds peak at 5-8 years. But for immediate pleasure, food pairing, and value, Alentejo offers tremendous appeal. Producers like Herdade do Rocim and Cortes de Cima deliver serious quality at accessible prices.

### Madeira: Oxidation and Time Create Magic

Madeira, produced on the volcanic island of Madeira off Portugal's coast, is unique: a fortified wine deliberately oxidized and aged in hot conditions, creating flavors of caramel, toasted nuts, dried fruit, and subtle acidity that seems impossible in a fortified wine.

**The Four Styles:** Madeira comes in four distinct categories, defined by grape and residual sugar. Sercial (the driest) shows pale gold color, bracing acidity, citrus, and almond—serve at 50-55°F as an aperitif. Verdelho (medium-dry) displays amber color, nuttiness, spice, and citrus—pair with light cheese or seafood. Bual (medium-sweet) shows rich brown color, honey, caramel, and dried fruit—pair with nutmeg-spiced desserts or blue cheese. Malmsey (the sweetest) is dark amber, nearly syrupy, with molasses, fig, and raisin—serve as dessert or meditation.

**Age Matters Enormously:** A 5-Year Madeira is young, showing fresh caramel and spice. A 10-Year Madeira is richer, more complex. A 15-Year or older Madeira is profound, with layers of flavor. Unlike vintage Port, Madeira improves indefinitely in the bottle; a 50-year-old Malmsey is not a curiosity—it's transcendent.

**Unique Advantage:** Madeira's oxidative nature means the bottle's shelf-life is essentially unlimited. Once opened, a bottle of Madeira can sit uncorked for months without degradation. This makes Madeira perfect for restaurants: open a bottle, serve by the glass for weeks.

## GREECE: Europe's Most Ancient Wine Region (And Most Modern Revolution)

Wine was born in Greece. Ancient Greek civilization built itself around wine, trade, and symposium. Yet for 400 years of Ottoman occupation and a century of phylloxera and civil conflict, Greek wine nearly disappeared. Only in the last 30 years has Greece rediscovered its winemaking identity. Understanding modern Greek wine is understanding a nation reclaiming its heritage—and producing genuinely great bottles.

### Santorini: Volcanic Assyrtiko—Minerality as Pure Concept

Perched 600 feet above the Aegean Sea on the island of Santorini, vineyards grow in one of Earth's most extreme environments: volcanic soil, fierce winds, minimal rainfall, intense sun. Yet from these brutal conditions emerges Assyrtiko—perhaps the world's purest expression of mineral winemaking.

**Terroir as Teacher:** Santorini's Assyrtiko shows what happens when a grape meets place so perfectly that the wine becomes inseparable from soil. The island's volcanic ash, pumice, and weathered lava create wines of laser-focused acidity (13.5-14.5% alcohol), bracing minerality, and saline precision. Flavors point toward citrus, green apple, sometimes tropical fruit, but always mineral-driven—seashell, flint, iodine. These wines don't seduce; they captivate through intellectual precision.

Assyrtiko improves for 5-8 years; a 2018 Assyrtiko is still evolving beautifully in 2026. The region's top producers—Santo Winery, Sigalas, Gaia Wines, Argyros—craft Assyrtikos that age like fine white Burgundy, developing toasted brioche and honeyed complexity with time.

**Food Pairing Perfection:** Santorini Assyrtiko is the world's finest wine for seafood, particularly raw preparations. Oysters, sea urchin, ceviche, crudo, grilled fish—Assyrtiko's minerality and acidity sing. Serve at 45-48°F.

**A Note on Oxidized Assyrtiko:** Some producers intentionally oxidize Assyrtiko (creating amber color and nutty character), challenging the region's conventional profile. These are fascinating, age-worthy experiments, but traditional Assyrtiko—pale, mineral, fresh—remains the classic expression.

### Naoussa: Xinomavro, Greece's Answer to Barolo

In the Naoussa region of northern Greece, high altitude (1,600-2,000 feet) and cool conditions create Xinomavro—a red grape of startling power and complexity. Xinomavro means "acid-black," literally describing the grape's high tannins and acidity. Wine professionals often call it "the Barolo of Greece," and the comparison is apt: both demand aging, both pair with serious food, both reveal profound complexity only with bottle time.

**Understanding Xinomavro's Structure:** Xinomavro naturally carries high acidity (typically 5.5-6.5 pH units) and firm tannins. Young Xinomavro can be austere, even unwelcoming. But patience transforms it. By year five, the wine begins showing secondary aromatics: dried herbs, mushroom, game, leather. By year ten, it achieves remarkable elegance—still tannic, but integrated, showing layers of complexity that rival aged Barolo or Bordeaux.

Naoussa's limestone-rich marl soils contribute mineral precision and structural definition. A serious Naoussa from a producer like Thymiopoulos, Kir-Yianni, or Alpha Estate demands five to ten years of aging before opening. A 2015 Naoussa is just entering its drinking window now.

**Food Pairing Mastery:** Xinomavro pairs with braised lamb, game birds, hard aged cheeses, mushroom risotto, and Mediterranean meat stews. The wine's acidity cuts through rich preparations while its tannins grip savory, umami-driven flavors.

### Nemea: Agiorgitiko, Greece's Most Approachable Red

In the Peloponnese, the Nemea region produces Agiorgitiko—"the grape of St. George." This red is more approachable than Xinomavro, typically showing fuller body, riper fruit, and softer tannins. Where Xinomavro demands a decade, Agiorgitiko often shows beautifully at 3-5 years.

**Flavor Profile and Food Pairing:** Agiorgitiko shows dark fruit (plum, blackberry), spice (black pepper, nutmeg), and sometimes mineral undertones. Alcohol often reaches 13.5-14.5%. The wines are generous, Mediterranean in spirit, food-friendly. Pair with grilled lamb, spiced meat preparations, aged cheese, or Mediterranean vegetable dishes.

Quality producers like Vaeni, Tsantali, and Kainourgio craft Agiorgitikos worthy of restaurant wine lists. A solid Nemea Agiorgitiko offers excellent value and approachability—ideal for wine drinkers transitioning into more serious Greek wines.

### Retsina: Not a Mistake, But a Tradition

Retsina—white or rosé wine infused with Aleppo pine resin—represents a 2,500-year-old tradition that confounds modern drinkers. Many encounter it as an accident (cheap tourist wine) and recoil. In truth, retsina is a deliberate, sophisticated expression of Greek winemaking culture.

**Historical Context:** In ancient Greece, amphoras were sealed with pine resin to prevent oxidation and spoilage. Over time, the resin's flavor became desirable—so desired that winemakers began adding resin intentionally. The tradition persisted through Ottoman occupation and survives today as an expression of Greek identity.

**Taste Profile:** Retsina shows piney, herbal, almost medicinal aromatics (in a fascinating way—not off-putting to those appreciating the tradition). The wine is typically dry, light-bodied, crisp, with high acidity. Most retsina is made from Savatiano grapes, though quality varies enormously.

**Modern Appreciation:** Contemporary retsina comes in two camps: traditional taverna-style retsina (often oxidized, rustic, low alcohol—12-13%) meant for immediate consumption with food, and modern-styled retsina (cleaner, fresher, more balanced). The latter represents retsina's future and deserves respect. A quality retsina from a producer like Bretanomyces or Papapetros, chilled to 45-48°F, pairs beautifully with Greek mezze, fresh seafood, or simply as a cultural exploration.

**Common Misconception:** Many assume retsina is sweet. Authentic retsina is bone-dry; any sweetness is secondary to resin character.

### Amyndeon and Cooler Regions: Xinomavro Reimagined

In cooler regions like Amyndeon (Macedonia), Xinomavro shows different expression than Naoussa: lighter body, more aromatic, less tannic. These wines drink younger (4-6 years) and offer approachability without sacrificing complexity. They're excellent for wine drinkers curious about Xinomavro but intimidated by Naoussa's austerity.

### Crete: Ancient Winemaking Reborn

Crete claims winemaking history stretching back 4,000 years—arguably the oldest continuous wine tradition on Earth. The island's warm climate and indigenous red grapes like Kotsifali and Mandilaria create wines of distinctive character: dark fruit, savory spice, herbal complexity, sometimes wild berry and soy sauce undertones (from Mandilaria's unique chemistry).

Cretan wines are increasingly recognized internationally. Producers like Douloufakis and Lyrarakis craft Cretan reds worthy of serious attention—ageworthy, complex, distinctly Mediterranean. A Cretan blend of Kotsifali and Mandilaria offers fascinating complexity and value.

### Greek Whites Beyond Assyrtiko

Greece produces excellent whites beyond Santorini. Agiorgitiko white (yes, the red variety has a white expression) shows crisp, citrus character. Moschofilero, grown in the cool Mantineia region of the Peloponnese, produces aromatic, delicate whites with floral and spice notes. Robola, from the Ionian island of Cephalonia, shows pronounced minerality and aging potential—a white capable of 10+ years development.

These wines remain underappreciated internationally but deserve exploration by serious wine professionals.

## Practical Wisdom: Serving, Storing, and Pairing

**Temperature Matters:** Spanish reds improve dramatically from proper serving temperature. A Rioja Reserva at 62-64°F shows far greater complexity than at room temperature (68-70°F). Greek reds (Xinomavro, Agiorgitiko) similarly benefit from 60-64°F service. Whites should be chilled but not ice-cold: 45-50°F for Albariño and Assyrtiko preserves mineral complexity that temperatures below 40°F suppress.

**Decanting Decisions:** Younger reds (Toro, young Ribera del Duero) benefit from one hour of decanting. Older bottles (Rioja Gran Reserva, aged Naoussa) improve dramatically with 90 minutes to two hours. Whites rarely need decanting.

**Storage Considerations:** Spanish Crianza and Portuguese Vinho Verde show best within 2-4 years. Rioja Reserva, Ribera del Duero, Priorat, and Dão improve for 10-25 years. Greek wines (especially Xinomavro and quality Assyrtiko) deserve patience: 5-10 years minimum.

**Value and Availability:** Spanish wines offer remarkable value across all categories. A $25 Rioja Reserva outperforms many $50 Bordeaux. Portuguese Douro reds and Dão deliver quality at 30% less than comparable Rhône wines. Greek wines, newly rediscovered, show extraordinary value: a serious Santorini Assyrtiko or Naoussa Xinomavro at $20-35 represents remarkable quality-to-price ratio.

## Conclusion: The Sommelier's Opportunity

These three nations represent centuries of wine tradition, thousands of indigenous grape varieties, and terroirs ranging from volcanic islands to granite mountains to slate-strewn hillsides. For wine professionals, understanding Spain, Portugal, and Greece is understanding wine's past, present, and future. These are wines worth studying, worth opening, worth sharing with clients. They reward knowledge with complexity, tradition with evolution, and patience with profound satisfaction.
